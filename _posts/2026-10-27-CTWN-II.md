Before we continue, a short recap of the functional analysis of tempered distributions. Consider $\mathcal{S} \equiv \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}^d)$, the space of vector-valued Schwartz functions i.e. the space of smooth functions $\phi \in \mathcal{C}^\infty(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}^d)$ that decay rapidly at infinity along with all their derivatives. Denote

$$p_{k, l\:}(\phi) \equiv \underset{1 \leq i \leq d}{\max} \; \underset{t \in \mathbb{R}}{\sup} \, \lvert t^k \, \frac{d^{\:l}}{dt^{\:l}} \phi_i(t) \rvert \;\;\;\; , \phi = (\phi_1, \dots, \phi_d) \in \mathcal{S}$$

a family of seminorms on $\mathcal{S}$, parameterized by indices $$k, l \in \mathbb{N}_0$$. Then $$\phi \in \mathcal{S}$$ is equivalent to $p_{k, l\:}(\phi) < +\infty$ for all $k, l \in \mathbb{N}_0$.

$\mathcal{S}$ with the above family of semi-norms is a Fréchet space i.e. it is a complete metrizable Hausdorff locally compact topological vector space (lctvs) (metrizability of a tvs implies the Hausdorff property). $\mathcal{S}$ is separable (consider functions with rational Taylor expansion around rational points) and therefore a Polish space. Note that because of the metrizability, being separable is equivalent to being second countable.

Be $\mathcal{S}^\prime \equiv \mathcal{S}^\prime(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}^d)$ the topological dual of $\mathcal{S}$ w.r.t. the Fréchet topology. $\mathcal{S}^\prime$ is called the space of vector-valued tempered distributions on $\mathbb{R}$. $\mathcal{S}^\prime$ with the $\sigma(\mathcal{S}^\prime, \mathcal{S})$- aka weak-* topology is a complete Hausdorff lctvs. It is separable but not second countable (not even first countable). Since $\mathcal{S}^\prime$ is non-metrizable, this is no contradiction.

$\mathcal{S}^\prime$ does have an LF-space structure. The topology induced by that is the strong dual topology, which is finer than $\sigma(\mathcal{S}^\prime, \mathcal{S})$. $\mathcal{S}^\prime$ with the strong dual topology is still a complete Hausdorff lctvs. The strong dual topology is non-metrizable and non-separable. But it is nuclear, which $\sigma(\mathcal{S}^\prime, \mathcal{S})$ is not.

The mapping $\mathcal{S} \, \ni \, \chi \longmapsto T_\chi \, \in \, \mathcal{S}^\prime$ with

$$ \langle T_\chi \mid \phi \rangle \equiv \int_{\mathbb{R}} {\chi(t)}^\top \phi \; dt \;\;\; \forall \phi \in \mathcal{S} $$

induces a natural injective homomorphism of $\mathcal{S}$ into its dual space $\mathcal{S}^\prime$. This embedding does not preserve the Fréchet topology on $\mathcal{S}$. $\mathcal{S}$ inherits the weak-* subspace topology of $\mathcal{S}^\prime$ .

The embedding $\mathcal{S} \, \hookrightarrow \, \mathcal{S}^\prime$ factors in a natural way over $L^2 \equiv L^2(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}^d)$ i.e.

$$ \mathcal{S} \, \overset{i}{\hookrightarrow} \, L^2 \, \overset{j}{\hookrightarrow} \, \mathcal{S}^\prime \;\;\;\; . $$

The injections are continuous homomorphisms of tvs.

While $\mathcal{S}$ is dense in $L^2$, the completion of $j(L^2)$ w.r.t. the induced topology is just $L^2$ again and not $\mathcal{S}^\prime$, so no density here. (There are lots of tempered distributions not originating from $L^2$, polynomials of $\delta$ for example).

$\mathcal{S}$ is sequentially dense in $\mathcal{S}^\prime$, i.e. for every tempered distribution $T \in \mathcal{S}^\prime$ there exists a sequence of test functions $\chi_n \in \mathcal{S}$ which converges to $T$ in the weak-* topology :

$$ \langle T \mid \phi \rangle = \underset{n \rightarrow +\infty}{\lim} \langle T_{\chi_n} \mid \phi \rangle \;\;\; \forall \phi \in \mathcal{S} $$
