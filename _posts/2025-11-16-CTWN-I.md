My first post (and a few more) will be about the surprisingly elaborate mathematically rigorous definition of continuous-time white noise.

A **discrete-time white noise process** with mean $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and positive semidefinite covariance matrix $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a stochastic process

$$
  \displaystyle W = {(W_n)}_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0} \;\;, \;\; W_n \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, P; \mathbb{R}^d)
$$

with constant mean $\mathbb{E}[W_n] = \mu$, constant covariance matrix $\mathrm{Cov}(W_n) = \Sigma$ and serially uncorrelated random variables i.e.

$$
  \mathrm{Cov}(W_n, W_m) = \delta_{nm} \, \Sigma \;\;\;\;, \forall n, m \in \mathbb{N}_0 \;\;\; .
$$

$W$ is by definition identically distributed but not i.i.d. (uncorrelatedness does not imply independence). In many use cases, being i.i.d. or even being Gaussian (which implies i.i.d.) is required of the $W_n$. We then speak of an i.i.d. or Gaussian discrete-time white noise process. A discrete-time white noise process is by definition strict stationary and WSS.

A plot of a univariate discrete-time white noise process with i.i.d. $W_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ shows the expected lack of temporal correlation. Compare this with the plot of an AR(1) process below.

A naive "pass to the limit" extrapolation from the definition of discrete-time white noise translates the uncorrelatedness condition into

$$
  \mathrm{Cov}(W_t,W_s) = \delta(t - s) \, \Sigma \;\;\;\;, \forall t, s \in \mathbb{R}_+
$$

for a now continuous-time \"white noise\" process $$W \equiv (W_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}_{+}}$$. $\delta$ is the Dirac delta distribution. This construction makes no sense for $W_t$ as random functions of $t$ but gives an intuition where to start instead. Defining $W$ as a generalized stochastic process of random distributions (aka random generalized functions) acting on test functions is where we are going next.
